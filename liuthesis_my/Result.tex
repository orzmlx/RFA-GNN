\chapter{Results}
This chapter presents the main results for the proposed model and the baseline models under the warm, cold drug target, and cold cell splits. The results are organized into error, correlation, top gene precision, direction of change, ablation, uncertainty, data efficiency, and case studies.

\section{Evaluation framework}
\noindent All experiments in this chapter use the L1000 landmark setting with 978 genes. For every sample, the prediction target is the response vector $y = x_{\mathrm{trt}} - x_{\mathrm{ctl}}$. All learned models use the matched control profile and the drug input. UPert also uses cell context information.

\noindent The evaluation compares DeepCOP \cite{woo2020deepcop} as the dense baseline, GSNN \cite{evans2024gsnn} as the graph based baseline, and the proposed model, named Uncertainty Perturbation (UPert), in two versions. The no-CF version is the standard UPert model without the counterfactual constraint. The CF version keeps the same architecture but adds the counterfactual drug constraint described in the methodology chapter. Some tables and figures also include simple mean baselines, namely mean global, mean cell, and mean drug.
% where the latter two use global fallback when the corresponding cell line or drug is unavailable in the training split.
% \noindent Most tables in this chapter report both UPert variants explicitly, while the benchmark figures usually keep the main no-CF visualization unless noted otherwise. Later sections then switch to additional analyses. The ablation section compares cell identity and control derived cell context within UPert. The uncertainty section studies the uncertainty output of the same UPert model. The data efficiency section studies warm split training budgets only. The case study section uses selected UPert examples to help explain the model behavior.

\section{Precision metric results}
This part examines whether the models recover the strongest changes across genes. The evaluation uses top gene precision, which emphasizes the genes with the largest positive and negative responses.

\noindent For each sample, the genes are ranked in both the true response ($y_{\mathrm{true}}$) and the predicted response ($y_{\mathrm{pred}}$). Positive precision at 20, written as Pos P@20, measures how many of the 20 most up regulated genes in $y_{\mathrm{pred}}$ also appear among the top 100 most up regulated genes in $y_{\mathrm{true}}$. Negative precision at 20, written as Neg P@20, is defined in the same way for the 20 most down regulated genes. The final score is the mean across all test samples. These metrics give another view of prediction quality that is closer to how perturbation results are often used in practice.

\noindent Table~\ref{tab:top_gene_analysis} reports the benchmark comparison with both UPert variants. In the latest exports, no-CF UPert remains stronger on top gene precision in warm and cold drug target, whereas CF UPert becomes stronger in cold cell on both Pos P@20 and Neg P@20.

\section{Ablation analysis}
\noindent This ablation study is separate from the main benchmark comparison. It includes two focused analyses. First part compares two ways of providing cell information to the proposed model. Another part examines the contribution of the drug target input by testing what happens when that input is removed at test time.

\subsection{Cell information ablation}
\noindent The two cell information variants are illustrated earlier in Figure~\ref{fig:cellid_embedding} and Figure~\ref{fig:cell_context}. Table~\ref{tab:context_ablation} compares them quantitatively. This ablation uses the CF enabled UPert setting, so the comparison isolates the form of cell information while keeping the counterfactual loss active in both variants. In the table, ``CellId'' means that the model receives only the cell line identity as a learned embedding, while ``Cell context'' means that it receives a context vector derived from the matched control profile. Here $\Delta$ means cell context minus CellId, so a negative $\Delta$MSE and a positive $\Delta$PCC both indicate improvement. The cell context variant improves both metrics in all three splits. The changes are modest in warm and cold drug target, but much larger in cold cell. The benefit of cell context appears in every split and is strongest when the test cell line is unseen.

\subsection{Drug target contribution}
\noindent This analysis examines the contribution of the drug target input more directly. The idea is simple. If the drug target vector carries useful information, performance should drop when that input is removed at test time. In this subsection, CF means the counterfactual constraint introduced in the method chapter. During training, that constraint compares the normal drug input with a counterfactual version in which the drug input is removed, in order to discourage the model from ignoring the drug signal. The comparison is shown in two steps. Table~\ref{tab:drug_zero_sanity_no_cf} compares the normal drug setting and the zero drug setting for the no-CF variant. Table~\ref{tab:drug_zero_sanity_cf} shows the same comparison for the CF variant. Here $\Delta$ is defined as zero drug minus test, so a positive $\Delta$MSE and a negative $\Delta$PCC both indicate performance degradation. The main pattern is that the CF variant is clearly more sensitive to removing the drug target input in all three splits, and the largest gap appears in cold cell. This gives direct evidence that the CF design makes the model depend more strongly on the perturbation signal rather than mainly on the baseline state. In the standard test setting, CF also improves both MSE and PCC in cold cell.

\section{Uncertainty analysis}
In UPert, the model predicts both a mean response and a variance term for each gene. The aim here is to examine whether that uncertainty output behaves like a reliability signal. In this section, $\mu$ denotes the predicted mean response, $\sigma^{2}$ denotes the predicted variance, and $\sigma$ is the standard deviation derived from that variance. For probabilistic models, the most direct quantitative metric is the test negative log likelihood, because it evaluates the predicted mean and variance together under the Gaussian output assumption. Table~\ref{tab:uncertainty_empirical_coverage} summarizes the main uncertainty diagnostics for all three splits from the uncertainty enabled no-CF UPert, including NLL, MSE, PCC, and coverage on the full test set and on the best NLL ranked subsets. Warm and cold cell again have similar full test NLL, while cold drug target is moderately higher rather than catastrophically worse. The full test coverage is close to 96\% in warm and cold cell, but lower at about 88.6\% in cold drug target. The rest of this section focuses on complementary uncertainty diagnostics through error relations, grouped uncertainty analysis, and coverage of prediction bands. Figure~\ref{fig:uncertainty_summary_placeholder} shows three views of this run, namely uncertainty averaged within each sample, uncertainty versus error, and grouped uncertainty analysis for warm, cold drug target, and cold cell.

\subsection{Uncertainty and MSE}
\noindent To check whether the model becomes more uncertain when it makes larger mistakes, each test sample is plotted by its uncertainty averaged within that sample and its MSE for that sample. In all three splits, the point clouds show a positive relationship. The trend is strongest in cold cell, moderate in warm, and weaker in cold drug target. When reading the middle upper panel of Figure~\ref{fig:uncertainty_summary_placeholder}, the key pattern is that higher uncertainty values are usually associated with higher MSE.

\subsection{Uncertainty and PCC}
\noindent A related analysis compares the same uncertainty value with PCC computed separately for each sample. This gives a different view from MSE. In Figure~\ref{fig:uncertainty_summary_placeholder}, only cold cell shows a clearly visible negative relationship. In warm and cold drug target, the relationship between uncertainty and PCC is weak and not clearly monotonic. The uncertainty output therefore follows absolute error more clearly than correlation measured within each sample.

\subsection{Uncertainty groups analysis}
\noindent The same pattern can be shown more directly by grouping test samples into low, medium, and high uncertainty groups. The groups are formed by sorting the samples according to the uncertainty averaged within each sample and splitting them into three equal sized parts. The average MSE and PCC are then calculated inside each group. In the right panel of Figure~\ref{fig:uncertainty_summary_placeholder}, the main pattern is clear for MSE in all three splits, since the high uncertainty group always has the largest mean error. For PCC, the separation is visible mainly in cold cell and much weaker in warm and cold drug target.

\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.92\linewidth]{figures/uncertainty_summary_placeholder.png}
\caption[Uncertainty Summary]{Uncertainty summary in warm, cold drug target, and cold cell, based on the test set. The left panel shows uncertainty averaged within each sample in the three splits. The middle panels compare uncertainty with MSE and PCC. The right panel shows MSE and PCC for low, medium, and high uncertainty groups in each split.}

\subsection{Prediction calibration}
\noindent Under a Gaussian interpretation, the band $\mu \pm 2\sigma$ can be read as an approximate 95\% prediction interval. To check this more directly, the empirical coverage is calculated as the fraction of target genes whose true values fall inside $\mu \pm 2\sigma$. Table~\ref{tab:uncertainty_empirical_coverage} reports the full test set together with the best top10 samples ranked by sample level NLL from low to high. This makes it possible to compare overall calibration with a best calibrated subset without overloading the table. In the uncertainty enabled no-CF UPert, warm and cold cell remain close to the nominal 95\% level on the full test set, while cold drug target is clearly lower. The best top10 subset is still fairly similar across all three splits. This suggests that cold drug target has weaker overall calibration than the other two splits.

\subsection*{Cold Drug Target Cases}
\noindent For the cold drug target analysis, the case studies are taken from the \texttt{cold\_target\_pattern} split. In this split, the held out test drugs do not share target genes or target patterns with the training drugs. The attention analysis therefore asks whether the model still gives readable high weight edges when the drug target information is fully unseen during training. The attention tables below are kept as qualitative examples for this split. In the current no-CF UPert benchmark used in this chapter, the same split reaches test MSE $1.2643$ and test PCC $0.5220$. \subsection*{Cold Cell Cases}
\noindent The cold cell test asks a different question. Instead of comparing different drugs, it asks whether the same drug keeps a readable set of high weight edges when the cell line is unseen. The most useful comparison in this setting is therefore not an overall heatmap across many drugs, but a direct warm versus cold cell comparison for the same drug.

\noindent Table~\ref{tab:attention_shared_drug_warm_cold_cell} compares the same drug, alvocidib labelled as \texttt{BRD-K87909389}, in warm and cold cell. This drug is reported as a \texttt{CDK inhibitor}. It is an appropriate example because the model performs well on this drug in both settings.

\noindent The table shows a shift in the highly weighted edges. In the warm setting, several top edges involve genes related to cell division, such as \texttt{RB1} and \texttt{E2F2}. In the cold cell setting, more top edges involve genes related to broader stress and damage response, such as \texttt{TP53} and \texttt{NR3C1}. When the cell line is unseen, the model gives more weight to broad response genes than to the more specific genes seen in the warm setting.

\noindent Overall, these case studies show that attention is most useful as a qualitative interpretation tool rather than as a stand alone measure of model quality. When the prediction is strong, the highly weighted edges are often easier to connect to known drug targets or to a later response program that looks biologically reasonable. When the prediction is weak, large edge weights can still appear, but they are harder to summarize in a meaningful way. The cold drug target examples show that readable high attention edges can still appear even when the true targets are fully unseen during training, but target proximity alone does not guarantee stronger prediction. The warm and cold cell comparisons answer a different question, namely whether the model retains a stable drug related subgraph when the cell background changes. For some drugs, the model shifts toward broader response genes in unseen cells. For other drugs, it keeps giving high weights to a more similar set of edges. Attention should therefore be interpreted together with prediction quality, sanity checks, and prior biological knowledge.
