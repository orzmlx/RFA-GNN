\noindent Drug development is slow, expensive, and risky. Many candidate drugs fail before they reach the market. One reason is that drug effects are hard to predict. A drug may bind to its target proteins, but the final cellular response depends on many connected genes, proteins, and signaling pathways. This makes it hard to predict how a drug perturbation changes gene expression in a specific cell type.

\noindent Large biological databases now provide information that can help with this task. Some databases describe molecular interactions, such as gene regulatory links and protein interactions. Other databases provide drug target annotations. These resources make it possible to combine perturbation data with prior biological knowledge.

\noindent This thesis studies a graph based model for predicting drug induced gene expression changes. The model is referred to as CAGNN, which stands for Counterfactual Attention GNN. It combines a molecular interaction network with drug target information. The network gives a structured view of how perturbation signals may move through the cell. Drug targets mark where the perturbation starts. The model takes the matched control profile as input and predicts the gene expression response after treatment.

\noindent The model is evaluated under warm, unseen drug, and unseen cell settings. It is compared with baseline models that do not use the biological graph. The results show that prior molecular knowledge and drug target information can improve perturbation prediction, especially in harder generalization settings. The learned attention weights also provide an interpretation signal for which molecular relations the model uses most strongly.
